01 / Understand before connecting

What must be checked before a prototype can communicate?

Start from the existing system

During my one-month internship in Orly, I joined the EL52 team with Mathéo Lopes Boto as my tutor. The team already had a bench for testing the aircraft Power Distribution Unit. My assignment was to study that system and develop a compact prototype for selected checks. The workbench photograph places this task in its practical electrical environment: equipment, connections and measurements.

Look beyond the software

The first question was how the electronics would connect to the equipment. I examined the PDU connections, including CANH, CANL and Case Ground, alongside the labelled power terminals. During the communication investigation, I checked wiring and the reference between the PDU case ground and the prototype GND. A possible receive-path problem had to be examined across the physical interface as well as the program.

Use measurements as evidence

I measured approximately 114 Ω between CANH and CANL. That reading was one observation to consider alongside configuration, wiring and grounding checks. It did not establish the condition of the entire communication chain. The lesson was to connect each software symptom with physical observations, then compare the evidence before deciding which part of the system needed further investigation.

02 / From a maintenance need to a prototype

How could selected PDU checks fit into a compact diagnostic tool?

Define a practical objective

The existing bench provided the starting point. The objective was a smaller, transportable device that would let a technician perform selected checks without using a computer. I developed the prototype architecture around an Arduino UNO R4 Minima with a Renesas RA4M1 microcontroller, an SN65HVD230-based transceiver—the interface linking the microcontroller to the CAN bus—and a 20×4 LCD connected through I²C. Each component served a different part of the diagnostic chain.

Turn the protocol into software

The PDU uses CAN 2.0B with extended 29-bit identifiers at 125 kbit/s. I worked on CAN initialization and developed a test-frame function using identifier 0x06000021 with DLC = 0, meaning no data bytes. The next questions concerned reception and interpretation: how to read a reply, separate its fields and turn raw bytes into information the user could understand. I explored field groupings while developing the embedded software.

Design for the technician

I developed an LCD menu with planned categories for identification, PCB status, faults, address pins, functional status and I1 / I2 / I3 information. The interface was intended to make those categories directly accessible. Useful diagnostic results depend on correctly received and interpreted replies, however. The menu and request software formed part of the prototype; reception became a central investigation within the project.

03 / Observe, compare and investigate

If the PDU was communicating, why was the prototype missing its replies?

Separate what was observed

The electrical cabinet photograph shows the industrial setting around the project. The debugging work concerned the PDU prototype: CAN initialization worked and TX activity was visible, but the UNO R4 did not correctly read the expected responses. A professional PCAN interface showed communication from the PDU. This comparison gave a reference observation and directed attention towards the prototype’s reception chain and its connection to the bus.

Build a testable investigation

The working hypothesis was that the difficulty could lie in the receiving interface or its configuration. I checked the 125 kbit/s setting, extended identifiers and CANH/CANL connections, together with TX/RX wiring, transceiver power, logic compatibility and bus termination. Shared ground and Case Ground were also examined. On the software side, I analysed frame reading and the behaviour of CAN.read(). These were possible contributing factors to investigate.

Keep the conclusion within the evidence

Visible TX activity alone did not establish a successful request-and-response exchange. The PCAN comparison showed that the PDU could communicate, while expected-reply reception on the prototype required further investigation. The practical outcome presented here is a structured diagnosis across hardware, protocol and software. The PCAN comparison provided a concrete reference for focusing the receive tests and continuing the investigation.

04 / Engineering within an aerospace workshop

What changes when classroom electronics meets aircraft maintenance?

Put the project in its setting

The engine photograph gives a wider view of the aeronautical maintenance environment at Air France Industries. My assigned project concerned the Power Distribution Unit and its diagnostic prototype. Working around real equipment connected an embedded-systems problem with a technician’s practical needs: a compact device, a readable interface and selected checks that could be carried out with simpler equipment.

Follow the whole information chain

The project linked skills that are often studied separately. Electrical measurements and ground references concerned the physical interface; CAN configuration and frame handling concerned communication; C/C++ and the LCD menu concerned software and the user interface. Comparing the prototype with PCAN brought those parts together. A question about a missing reply could require checking connections, protocol settings and program behaviour within the same investigation.

Learn from the scope of the work

The work covered system analysis, prototype architecture, test-frame software, LCD menu development and CAN reception troubleshooting. A complete diagnostic sequence would require reliable replies and their interpretation; that was the target behind the interface. For me, the experience made the connection between electronics, embedded systems and aeronautics concrete. It strengthened my interest in aerospace applications and in engineering methods based on observation, comparison and careful interpretation.
