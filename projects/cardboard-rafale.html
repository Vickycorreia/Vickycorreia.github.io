Material

The documents combine reference views with my own measurements, sketches and annotations. Their purpose is practical: give me something to refer to while tracing and cutting, and keep track of the relationships between parts.

01 / MY WORKING PLANS

From a photo.To a project.

Reclaimed cardboard, a reference photograph, and the desire to make something myself. My drawings and handwritten notes turn that idea into a model I can build, piece by piece.

Behind this step

The idea behind the plans

I started with an aircraft I wanted to understand through making. Rather than follow a ready-made assembly kit, I prepared my own working plans and annotations from visual references. The question is simple: how can a flat image become a recognisable volume in cardboard? I use lengths, proportions and several views to organise the nose, cockpit, wings and rear section. The sheets shown here keep those decisions visible. They also help me separate the overall outline from the smaller details that can wait. This remains a personal build in progress: the aim is to make each next step manageable, with a cutter, a hot glue gun and patience.

02 / MEASURE, TRACE, COMPARE

Keeping the measurements useful

Visible on the sheet
Top, side and front views with handwritten dimensions and guide lines.
What I check
Relative lengths, left/right placement and the relationship between the different views.
Purpose
Practical references for a handmade model, rather than a validated manufacturing drawing.

The coloured annotations help separate individual measurements from the printed reference outline. I can return to the same drawing when considering a wing position or the width of a section. The notes are part of my own preparation; they are not a claim that the model reproduces every aircraft detail exactly.

The point to watch is consistency: comparing two parts only makes sense when they refer to the same view and the same proportion system. For this project, a clear centre line and a few readable dimensions are more useful than adding detail that I cannot transfer reliably to the cardboard.

04 / WINGS & SYMMETRY

A simple way to check the shape

View
The current model from above, with delta wings and forward canards visible.
Reference
The longitudinal centre line and comparisons between the left and right sides.
What this shows
The shape of a handmade display model; no flight or aerodynamic validation.

A canard is the smaller forward surface positioned ahead of the main wing. In this model, the paired canards and broad delta wings are important visual features. Their presence helps make the aircraft recognisable without requiring every small detail to be reproduced immediately.

I can assess the outline with straightforward observations: look down the centre line, compare the outer edges and check how each part meets the body. The joints and visible layered edges will need to be considered alongside the future smoothing work, rather than hidden by colour alone.

Behind this step

What remains on the fuselage

From the side, the construction is easy to read: the pointed nose, raised cockpit, segmented body and tall rear fin form a continuous outline, while the surface still shows how the pieces meet. That distinction matters to me. A recognisable aircraft shape is already visible, but the maquette is not finished. Before aiming for an even grey appearance, I still need to consider the steps between panels, exposed edges and changes in curvature. The question is how to smooth the outside without losing the shape I have built. I have not selected a final finishing method yet. This next stage will need the same patience as the cutting and assembly, one area at a time.
