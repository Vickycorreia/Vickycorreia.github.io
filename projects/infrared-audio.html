From sound to light.

How can audio become an optical signal, then sound again?

The intended system is stereo: the left and right channels each need a transmitter and receiver chain, using different carriers. We are starting with one channel. The audio voltage should control a VCO frequency, whose output then drives an infrared LED. At the receiving end, the sensor current must be converted to voltage and processed before PLL demodulation and audio power amplification.

The challenge is connecting blocks that handle different quantities: audio voltage, frequency, LED current and then a very small received current. We check each interface before assembling the complete path. The whiteboard is a working architecture; it does not establish that audio recovery, stereo playback or remote volume control already works.

Current limiting

First approximation: R_LED ≈ (V_supply − V_F)/I_LED. For a saturated transistor, also account for V_CE,sat: R_LED ≈ (V_supply − V_F − V_CE,sat)/I_LED.

Driving the light.

Does the transistor drive the LED at a suitable current?

For this first test, a square wave replaces the future frequency-modulated source. It drives the transistor stage, which controls rapid LED switching. The experiment is deliberately simple: before transmitting music, we need to check that electrical commands produce the intended emission and that the current suits the device.

The resistor calculation depends on the supply voltage, LED forward voltage and chosen current. About 10 mA was selected as a cautious initial design target, but the actual current still needs measurement. The visible red LED probably acts as an indicator; the clear LED and transistor references must be recorded to confirm their functions and refine the design.

Making the signal visible.

How can we obtain a readable signal without saturating the amplifier?

The sensor converts received light into electrical current. That current must become a usable voltage and then be amplified. We use a TL081 teaching board to observe it on the oscilloscope. External resistors, capacitors and an adjustable setting help explore the stage; their exact functions must be checked against its actual schematic.

More gain is not always better: excessive gain can saturate the output, while an unsuitable RC network may show only fast signal changes. We therefore need to record the wiring, check supply voltages and offset, then compare input and output at a fixed frequency and amplitude. The photograph does not establish that the board already implements the transimpedance front end envisaged for the final system.

Experiment · Questions · Next measurements

Connecting the two blocks.

Does the response actually depend on the light path?

The setup combines the transistor stage and TL081 receiver. A function generator supplies the test signal, while the instruments track the command and the response observed at the receiver. The immediate question is practical: can we drive emission and obtain an electrical response after detection and amplification?

Interpreting that response requires control of sensor position, distance, ambient light and measurement nodes. A useful next experiment is to hold settings constant and vary just one parameter: alignment, distance or interruption of the light path. This also helps distinguish an optical response from possible electrical coupling. These checks are proposed next steps; the final range has not been measured.

Understanding the distortion.

Photographed test

10 kHz corresponds to a 100 µs period. The yellow trace is likely the command; the exact pink-trace measurement node still needs identification.

Proposed carrier

45 kHz corresponds to a calculated period of about 22.2 µs. This frequency has not yet been validated for the stereo FM chain.

Carson’s rule

B ≈ 2(Δf + f_audio,max). Audio bandwidth and deviation must be chosen before validating carriers and their separation.

Separating the channels

A first criterion is |f_c,2 − f_c,1| > (B₁ + B₂)/2, plus a guard band appropriate to the filters. The second carrier has not yet been selected.

Hypotheses to test

Possible causes of distortion are not established diagnoses. Identify the probe node, check gain and offset, then record frequency response to distinguish them.

Understanding the distortion.

Why does the received waveform look like a pulse?

The oscilloscope shows a square yellow trace and a pulse-like pink trace associated with the receiving chain. The test account places this experiment at 10 kHz: this is the intermediate test frequency, distinct from the proposed 45 kHz carrier. The received shape therefore differs from the transmitted shape. That is a useful observation, but it does not establish validated audio transmission.

Several explanations remain possible: capacitive coupling, an RC time constant, limited bandwidth, saturation, gain settings or the measurement node itself. We cannot select one cause from the photograph alone. Next we need the actual schematic and the precise pink-trace node, followed by a progressive 10, 20, 30 and 45 kHz sweep. Comparing amplitude and waveform will help identify what the stage passes or attenuates before introducing FM.

+ More information / Understanding FM

A carrier oscillates around a mean frequency fᶜ. The useful signal moves its instantaneous frequency around that mean: closely spaced cycles represent a higher frequency, and wider spacing represents a lower frequency. Here the carrier amplitude stays equal to 1; the β slider controls the modulation index, not that amplitude.

For a sinusoidal signal: s(t) = sin(2π fᶜ t + β sin(2π fᵐ t)).

Frequencies use relative units. The carrier is 16; maximum deviation is β × fᵐ. The eight notes played by “Hear the principle” are an audible illustration of frequency changes.

This demonstration explains the FM principle. It does not reproduce the circuit components, actual frequencies, stereo link or PLL. The infrared project remains in progress; these curves do not establish any additional experimental result.
