04 / Granular mechanics · Scientific study

Small grains.
Big questions.

When does a pile of sand become an avalanche? Why can it flow at an almost steady speed?

My study connects experiments, video measurements and physical models—from a rotating drum to a ball travelling over a row of tubes—to explore how granular materials start, move and settle.

The scientific question

Solid at rest.
Flowing in motion.

Sand consists of individual grains, yet a whole layer can behave like a solid or a flowing material. Its response depends on friction, collisions, slope, layer depth and its previous state.

The project investigates that transition through four connected experiments: characteristic angles in a drum, avalanches on a rough inclined plane fed by a hopper, the Tac-Tac model of repeated impacts, and the influence of sand thickness on stability.

01 / Onset, rest & friction

Two angles.
One memory.

Onset angle
θa = 36.7 ± 0.3° · reported measurement
Resting angle
θr = 32.1 ± 0.6° · reported measurement
Model input
Friction coefficient μ = 0.7

What the experiment studies

Rotating a partly filled drum raises the slope of the sand surface. An avalanche starts at a higher angle; after the grains move, the surface settles at a lower resting angle.

Why history matters

This difference illustrates hysteresis. Between the two thresholds, whether sand rests or flows depends on its previous state. Starting motion and stopping motion follow different paths.

The Coulomb model

The static-friction condition is T ≤ μN, where T is the tangential force and N the normal force. At the limiting angle:

θc = arctan(μ) ≈ 35° for μ = 0.7

This is a simplified model prediction, compared with the measured onset of 36.7°. The drum experiment also reveals a separate resting threshold.

Reported results and model: study presentation, slides 4–6. The ± values are reproduced as reported.

Creating an observable avalanche

The project included building an inclinable plane and a hopper to regulate the sand supply. The roughened base helps study flow over a granular surface, while the adjustable feed allows investigation of how supply affects the avalanche.

From video to motion

Point-tracking software follows the observed motion through successive frames. Position versus time makes it possible to estimate speed. In the reported avalanche observation, that speed was approximately steady at 30 cm/s.

A model that raises another question

A sliding-block model with Coulomb friction gives:

dv/dt = g(sin θ − μ cos θ)

For a slope above its friction threshold, this model predicts acceleration. That differs from the almost steady motion observed here, motivating a closer look at energy losses through repeated impacts.

03 / Impacts & energy losses

One grain.
Repeated impacts.

Analogue
A ball travelling over a row of cylinders
Tracked speed
Approximately 0.33 m/s · Tac-Tac run
Model estimate
Effective restitution coefficient e ≈ 0.46

Isolate a mechanism

The Tac-Tac apparatus models one grain and its contacts with its surroundings. A ball repeatedly meets the cylinders beneath it, gaining energy as it descends and losing energy at impacts.

The simplified impact law

The model uses vafter = e vbefore. The restitution coefficient e expresses how much speed is retained at a collision; the corresponding kinetic-energy loss depends on 1 − e².

Balance gains and losses

A steady-cycle balance between gravitational energy gained and energy dissipated gives a characteristic limiting speed:

vlim = √[2gd sin θ / (1 − e²)]

Here g is gravitational acceleration, θ the slope angle and d the spacing between successive support contacts. The speed can still vary during each step between impacts.

Compare with tracking

The reported position–time fit gives about 0.33 m/s. Applying the model gives e ≈ 0.46 for this analogue. Further tests examine the predicted dependence on √(sin θ). This estimate describes the Tac-Tac experiment.

Model and comparison: study presentation, slides 11–14 and 21. The restitution value is inferred through the model.

04 / A collective behaviour

Layer depth.
Stability limits.

Comparison
Two same-width flows with different depths
Hstart
Layer-thickness threshold for starting flow
Hstop
Deposited layer thickness when flow stops

Compare different depths

The experiment compares two sand flows of the same width but different thicknesses. In these trials, the thicker layer was less stable, showing that the slope alone does not describe the behaviour.

Starting and stopping

The Hstart / Hstop investigation relates layer thickness to the inclination at flow onset and arrest. These thresholds extend the hysteresis question from characteristic angles to the depth of the material.

Connect with previous research

The presentation compares the trends with a figure attributed to Adrian Daerr’s thesis. That figure serves as a literature reference for interpreting granular-flow stability.

The experimental limits

Reproducing the sand supply and initial conditions was difficult. Surface waves affected measurements, and internal forces and grain velocities were hard to access. A small laboratory apparatus also has limits when interpreting natural avalanches.

Thickness study and limitations: study presentation, slides 15–18. The supplied montages illustrate the experiments; reported numerical results come from the presentation.

More information · methodology & limits

Different experiments, different roles

The rotating drum was used to identify the characteristic angles. The inclinable plane and hopper supported avalanche observations; the Tac-Tac ball experiment explored repeated impacts at the scale of an isolated grain.

The observed sand avalanche moved at approximately 0.30 m/s. The separate Tac-Tac analysis used approximately 0.33 m/s; the two values describe different experiments.

What limited the measurements

  • Reproducing the same avalanche conditions and controlling sand flow rate.
  • Surface waves that affected measurements.
  • Laboratory scale and difficulty measuring dynamic quantities.
