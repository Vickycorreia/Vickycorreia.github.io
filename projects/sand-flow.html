04 / Granular mechanics · Scientific study

Small grains.
Big questions.

When does a pile of sand become an avalanche? Why can it flow at an almost steady speed?

My study connects experiments, video measurements and physical models—from a rotating drum to a ball travelling over a row of tubes—to explore how granular materials start, move and settle.

The scientific question

Solid at rest.
Flowing in motion.

Sand consists of individual grains, yet a whole layer can behave like a solid or a flowing material. Its response depends on friction, collisions, slope, layer depth and its previous state.

The project investigates that transition through four connected experiments: characteristic angles in a drum, avalanches on a rough inclined plane fed by a hopper, the Tac-Tac model of repeated impacts, and the influence of sand thickness on stability.

01 / Onset, rest & friction

Two angles.
One memory.

Onset angle
θa = 36.7 ± 0.3° · reported measurement
Resting angle
θr = 32.1 ± 0.6° · reported measurement
Model input
Friction coefficient μ = 0.7

What the experiment studies

Rotating a partly filled drum raises the slope of the sand surface. An avalanche starts at a higher angle; after the grains move, the surface settles at a lower resting angle.

Why history matters

This difference illustrates hysteresis. Between the two thresholds, whether sand rests or flows depends on its previous state. Starting motion and stopping motion follow different paths.

The Coulomb model

The static-friction condition is T ≤ μN, where T is the tangential force and N the normal force. At the limiting angle:

θc = arctan(μ) ≈ 35° for μ = 0.7

This is a simplified model prediction, compared with the measured onset of 36.7°. The drum experiment also reveals a separate resting threshold.

Reported results and model: study presentation, slides 4–6. The ± values are reproduced as reported.

How the experiment works

Watch the surface tilt.

Why does sand start moving at one angle, but stop at another?

A simple setup

A partly filled, transparent drum makes the question visible with very little equipment. Turning it changes the slope of the sand surface until the grains begin to slide. Once the avalanche ends, the surface settles again. The idea is to compare those two moments: the angle just before motion starts and the angle left after the sand comes to rest. One simple arrangement reveals two different thresholds.

What needs checking

The important precaution is to distinguish the first movement from the final resting surface. A point to check is whether the chosen observation captures the same event each time, rather than a brief movement of only a few grains. Small differences between readings also matter. The reported angles include uncertainties. Keeping them visible helps us compare the model and the measurements without treating the observed thresholds as exact.

What the observation tells us

The reported onset angle was 36.7 ± 0.3°, while the resting angle was 32.1 ± 0.6°. Starting and stopping therefore followed different paths: between the thresholds, the earlier state matters. This is the idea behind hysteresis. A simple friction model, using a coefficient of 0.7, predicted about 35°. Comparing that prediction with the measurements helps us assess what the model explains and why the separate resting threshold needs attention.

Creating an observable avalanche

The project included building an inclinable plane and a hopper to regulate the sand supply. The roughened base helps study flow over a granular surface, while the adjustable feed allows investigation of how supply affects the avalanche.

From video to motion

Point-tracking software follows the observed motion through successive frames. Position versus time makes it possible to estimate speed. In the reported avalanche observation, that speed was approximately steady at 30 cm/s.

A model that raises another question

A sliding-block model with Coulomb friction gives:

dv/dt = g(sin θ − μ cos θ)

For a slope above its friction threshold, this model predicts acceleration. That differs from the almost steady motion observed here, motivating a closer look at energy losses through repeated impacts.

How the experiment works

Make the motion visible.

Why does the avalanche keep a nearly steady speed instead of continually speeding up?

A simple setup

The arrangement combines an adjustable inclined plane, a rough base and a hopper that supplies sand. It turns an avalanche into something we can observe in the laboratory. The slope and sand supply give us clear questions to investigate. A video then records the movement, and point tracking follows its position through successive images. Comparing the distance travelled with the elapsed time gives an estimate of the observed speed.

What needs checking

Following a moving patch of sand is different from following one solid object. A point to check is whether the same feature is being tracked from one image to the next. The recording must also let us relate position to time consistently. These precautions help estimate the speed of the visible motion while accounting for tracking limits and differences between individual grains.

What the observation tells us

The reported avalanche travelled at approximately 30 cm/s, with an almost steady speed. A simple model of sliding with friction instead predicted acceleration above its threshold. This difference creates a useful scientific question: where is the energy going as the sand descends? It motivated the separate Tac-Tac experiment, which isolates repeated impacts. The observation gives a reason to examine another mechanism, while leaving the full behaviour of the sand layer open to investigation.

03 / Impacts & energy losses

One grain.
Repeated impacts.

Analogue
A ball travelling over a row of cylinders
Tracked speed
Approximately 0.33 m/s · Tac-Tac run
Model estimate
Effective restitution coefficient e ≈ 0.46

Isolate a mechanism

The Tac-Tac apparatus models one grain and its contacts with its surroundings. A ball repeatedly meets the cylinders beneath it, gaining energy as it descends and losing energy at impacts.

The simplified impact law

The model uses vafter = e vbefore. The restitution coefficient e expresses how much speed is retained at a collision; the corresponding kinetic-energy loss depends on 1 − e².

Balance gains and losses

A steady-cycle balance between gravitational energy gained and energy dissipated gives a characteristic limiting speed:

vlim = √[2gd sin θ / (1 − e²)]

Here g is gravitational acceleration, θ the slope angle and d the spacing between successive support contacts. The speed can still vary during each step between impacts.

Compare with tracking

The reported position–time fit gives about 0.33 m/s. Applying the model gives e ≈ 0.46 for this analogue. Further tests examine the predicted dependence on √(sin θ). This estimate describes the Tac-Tac experiment.

Model and comparison: study presentation, slides 11–14 and 21. The restitution value is inferred through the model.

  • Restitution
  • Energy balance

How the experiment works

One ball. One clear question.

Could repeated small impacts explain why the motion stops accelerating?

A simple setup

A ball moves over a row of inclined tubes, meeting one support after another. This simple arrangement stands in for one grain interacting with its surroundings. As the ball descends, gravity gives it energy; each impact can remove some of that energy. Isolating this repeated sequence makes the question easier to study than a whole avalanche. The aim is to explore one possible mechanism with a clearly visible, separate experiment.

What needs checking

A point to check is the difference between the ball’s motion within one step and its average progress over several contacts. It can speed up between impacts and lose speed at the next contact. Tracking position over time helps compare that overall movement with the model. Another precaution is to keep the interpretation limited to this apparatus: a ball and tubes simplify the much more complicated contacts inside real sand.

What the observation tells us

The Tac-Tac analysis used a tracked speed of about 0.33 m/s. Applying the impact model gave an effective rebound factor of about 0.46 for this analogue. That value was inferred through the model, rather than measured directly for all sand grains. The balance between energy gained during descent and energy lost at contacts offers a possible explanation for a characteristic speed. This result belongs to the ball experiment, separately from the sand avalanche.

Compare different depths

The experiment compares two sand flows of the same width but different thicknesses. In these trials, the thicker layer was less stable, showing that the slope alone does not describe the behaviour.

Starting and stopping

The Hstart / Hstop investigation relates layer thickness to the inclination at flow onset and arrest. These thresholds extend the hysteresis question from characteristic angles to the depth of the material.

Connect with previous research

The presentation compares the trends with a figure attributed to Adrian Daerr’s thesis. That figure serves as a literature reference for interpreting granular-flow stability.

The experimental limits

Reproducing the sand supply and initial conditions was difficult. Surface waves affected measurements, and internal forces and grain velocities were hard to access. A small laboratory apparatus also has limits when interpreting natural avalanches.

How the experiment works

Change one thing: the depth.

At the same slope, does adding more sand make the layer less stable?

A simple setup

Two flows of the same width, with different sand depths, provide a direct comparison. The basic idea is easy to read: change the amount of material and watch how its stability changes. A related experiment asks how thick a layer must be before it starts flowing, and how much remains when it stops. Together, these arrangements extend the earlier angle question to the amount of sand involved in the motion.

What made it difficult

The presentation reports difficulty reproducing the same starting conditions and controlling the sand supply. Surface waves also affected the measurements, while grain speeds and forces inside the layer were hard to access. These limits matter because a difference between trials can come from more than the intended change in depth. The small laboratory scale adds another boundary: the apparatus helps study mechanisms, but cannot reproduce every feature of a natural avalanche.

What the observation tells us

In the compared flows, the thicker layer was less stable. The result shows why the slope alone does not describe the behaviour: the amount of sand also matters. Studying the depths at which flow starts and stops connects this observation with the memory of motion. The presentation also uses a figure from Adrian Daerr’s thesis as a research reference. It supports the discussion without becoming a measurement made by our project.

Different experiments, different roles

The rotating drum was used to identify the characteristic angles. The inclinable plane and hopper supported avalanche observations; the Tac-Tac ball experiment explored repeated impacts at the scale of an isolated grain.

The observed sand avalanche moved at approximately 0.30 m/s. The separate Tac-Tac analysis used approximately 0.33 m/s; the two values describe different experiments.

  • Reproducing the same avalanche conditions and controlling sand flow rate.
